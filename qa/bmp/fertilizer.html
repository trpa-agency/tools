Fertilizer at Lake Tahoe

Lake Tahoe is clear because its water is naturally poor in nutrients. Fertilizer that washes off a lawn ends that.

Why fertilizer matters here

Lake Tahoe is ultra-oligotrophic: naturally very low in nutrients, which keeps algae and suspended particles low and the water exceptionally clear. Phosphorus and nitrogen from fertilizer, stormwater runoff, and soil disturbance feed algae and reduce clarity. Urban land contributes a large share of the phosphorus reaching the lake, and reducing fertilizer runoff is part of the Lake Tahoe Total Maximum Daily Load strategy for restoring clarity.

Tahoe soils already hold enough phosphorus for turf for many years. Fertilizer applied to landscapes, especially products containing phosphorus, washes into groundwater and stormwater and on to the lake, where it supports algae on nearshore rocks and the growth of invasive aquatic plants. Fertilizer is restricted in stream environment zones under Chapter 60 of the TRPA Code of Ordinances, and larger landscaped projects need a fertilizer management plan (template in Chapter 5 of the BMP Handbook).

How to fertilize without feeding the lake

Choose the right product

  • Zero phosphorus. The label shows a nutrient ratio, N-P-K. The middle number is P. Make sure it is 0.
  • Slow-release products for established lawns, perennials, shrubs, and trees.
  • Organic compost, mixed 6 to 12 inches deep, improves soil structure and holds nutrients where roots can use them.

Time it

  • Spring, after the snowpack has melted, in May.
  • Late summer, in September, when temperatures are cooling.
  • Only when plants are actively growing, and never when rain is in the forecast.

Use the right amount

  • Measure the lawn or garden (length times width) to get the square footage.
  • Turf needs one-half to three-quarters of a pound of nitrogen per 1,000 square feet per year in total. Use half to three-quarters of the rate on the bag.
  • Test the soil before fertilizing and adjust to the result. University extension offices and soil labs offer the service; home kits give a rougher answer.

Water it in, not off

  • Apply when the soil is moist and temperatures are moderate.
  • Water lightly so nutrients move into the root zone: 10 to 15 minutes on, an hour or two off, then repeat.
  • If you see runoff or pooling, stop and let the soil absorb what is there.

Think beyond the bag

  • Favor native and adapted plants that need little or no fertilizer once established.
  • Keep fertilizer away from streams, the shoreline, and other sensitive areas.
  • If a landscaping service does the work, give them these guidelines.

Why it works: phosphorus is the nutrient that, in excess, drives algae and aquatic plant growth. Every landscape choice, from product and timing to how you water, changes how much of it reaches the lake.
